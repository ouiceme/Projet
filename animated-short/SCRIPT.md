# "The Wave That Outran the Horses" — Molasses Flood, Boston 1919

## Facts (sources)
- 15 January 1919, Commercial Street, Boston North End.
- A 2.3-million-gallon molasses tank burst at 12:40 PM; it was 50 feet tall and 90 feet wide.
- The molasses wave was estimated at 25 feet high and moved at 35 mph — fast enough to outrun horses.
- The wave destroyed an elevated railway, crushed buildings, and killed 21 people; 150 were injured.
- The warm January day (40 °F / 4 °C) had raised the temperature of the cold, recently delivered Cuban molasses,
  dropping its viscosity and raising the internal pressure — combined with a poor steel tank and rivet failure.
- Rescue workers and victims were coated in molasses; the harbour ran brown for weeks.
- Cause: thermal expansion of the molasses inside a poorly constructed, never properly inspected tank.
- Physics analogy: a balloon full of water left in the sun — heat makes the liquid expand, but the skin doesn't.
  (Wikipedia: Great Molasses Flood)

## Script (~203 words, ~55 s at 3.7 w/s)

A wave of molasses once rolled through Boston at thirty-five miles an hour.

It's January 1919, and a massive steel tank on Commercial Street is full to the brim
with two-and-a-half million gallons of syrup.

The day is unseasonably warm, and the cold Cuban molasses inside starts to heat up.

Warm liquid expands. The tank does not.

A rivet pops. Then another. Then the whole side of the tank tears open in one enormous groan.

A wall of molasses twenty-five feet high sweeps down the street,
fast enough to outrun the horses tied at the kerb.

It flattens buildings. It buries an elevated railway. It drowns twenty-one people.

Luckily, the harbour was right there to drain it.
Unluckily, the harbour ran brown for months.

You see, liquids push harder on their container the warmer they get.
Like a water balloon left in the sun: the water swells, but the skin stays the same size.
Something has to give.

That tank had never been pressure-tested. Not once.

After the flood, Massachusetts passed the first modern construction safety laws in America.

So the next time you smell molasses on a Boston summer day, that is not your imagination.
